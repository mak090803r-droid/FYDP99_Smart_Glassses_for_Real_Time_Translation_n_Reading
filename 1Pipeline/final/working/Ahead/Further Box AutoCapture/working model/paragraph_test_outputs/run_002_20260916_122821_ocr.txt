EN16-02 - Planning a Small Observatory Night Shift The university astronomy club planned a four-hour observing session for 21 October 2026 at a hilltop site outside the city. The goal was not to collect publishable research data, but to train new members in safe telescope setup. sky navigation and log keeping. Sunset was expected shortly after 17:40, so the equipment team scheduled arrival for 16:50. This left enough daylight to inspect tripods, route power cables and confirm that the emergency lamps worked before the observing area became dark. Weather forecasts from three services disagreed about the amount of high cloud. One predicted 20% cloud cover, another 45%, and the local station suggested thin cloud would arrive after 21:00. The club therefore chose targets that could be observed in a flexible order. Bright objects. including Jupiter and several double stars, were placed early in the plan. Fainter nebulae were listed as optional. A small digital thermometer and hygrometer would record conditions every twenty minutes, because dew formation on optical surfaces was considered a greater operational risk than moderate high cloud. Roles and Safety Checks New members were divided into three pairs. One pair handled the 200 mm reflector, one operated the smaller refractor, and the third maintained the written observing log and checked red-light discipline. Every forty minutes the roles would rotate. Nobody was allowed to move a tripod without first announcing the movement, and food was kept away from the optical tables. The organizers also marked a two-metre clear path between the instruments and the vehicles so that people could leave safely without stepping over cases or cables. The session plan included a simple cancellation rule. If wind remained above 35 km/h for ten minutes, if rain was detected, or if visibility on the access road became poor, the group would pack immediately. A successful night was defined as safe practice and complete logs, not the number of objects seen By setting that expectation in advance, the club reduced pressure to continue in marginal conditions and gave beginners a structured introduction to field observing. A paper checklist at the counted before the group left the site
