EN12-03 - Digitizing Handwritten Field Notebooks A small environmental archive began digitizing field notebooks created by survey teams beween occasional measurements written in pencil. Because the originals were unique the project was designed around preservation first and speed second. Every notebook received a temporary work number, and staff photographed its cover and condition before scanning Pages with loose inserts were flagged so that no item would be lost when the binding was opened The scanning station used an overhead camera rather than a flalbed scanner because several bindings could not be pressed safely Pages were supported at a gentle angle and illuminaled by two diffuse lamps positioned to reduce glare A grey reference card appeared in the first image of each session Most pages were captured at 400 ppi equivalent resolution, while maps and pages containing very small annotations were recorded at higher resolution Operators checked focus al the centre and corners before continuing through a notebook. File naming was deliberately simple A notebook identified as FN-1994-17 produced images such as FN-1994-17_P001 and FN-1994-17_P002 If a loose insert belonged between pages 18 and 19, it received a suffix rather than forcing the rest of the sequence to be renamed A checksum was generated after transfer to the archive server, and a second copy was wriiten to offline storage The project log recorded the operator, capture date, equipment settings and any unusual page condition Access Copy Versus Original Evidence Text recognition was treated as an access aid, not a replacement for the image. Handwriting quality varied widely, and technical abbreviations often confused automated systems. Staff therefore created a short verified transcription only for titles, dates, locations and selected measurements Search users could find a notebook through those fields and then inspect the original page image Where a number was uncertain, the transcription included a visible uncertainty marker rather than silenily choosing a value. By the end of the first month. 2,480 pages had been captured from thirty-six notebooks. The team intentionally avoided setting a daily page quota because fragile material required different handling times. Instead, quality was reviewed through weekly sample checks of focus. sequence, colour reference and file integrity This slower but controlled workflow preserved the relationship between the digital files and the physical notebooks, which was more important than maximizing the number of scans completed each day
