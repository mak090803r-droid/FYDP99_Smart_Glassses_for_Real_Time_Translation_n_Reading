EN16-01- Calibrating Portable Air-Quality Sensors A teaching laboratory received twelve portable air-quality monitors for a field course on indoor ventilation. Before students could use them, the instruments had to be checked against the laboratory reference station. The monitors measured carbon dioxide, temperature and relative humidity, and they were expected to agree closely enough for comparative surveys. On 8 September 2026, the technician placed all units on the same bench for forty minutes so that their temperature could stabilize, Windows were kept closed, no one stood directly beside the sensors, and the reference station logged one reading every 30 seconds Morning Calibration Sequence The first comparison showed that most carbon-dioxide readings were within 35 ppm of the reference, but two monitors differed by more than 90 ppm. Their humidity channels also responded slowly after a brief change in room conditions. Rather than applying a correction immediately, the technician inspected the intake openings and found a thin layer of packing dust on both units. After cleaning the vents with dry air and repeating the stabilization period, the large offsets disappeared. The event was recorded because it demonstrated that a poor result can come from handling and storage rather than from a failed sensing element For the final check, the room was occupied by six students for fifteen minutes, raising the carbon-dioxide level from approximately 510 ppm to 1,120 ppm. Each monitor had to follow the rise and fall without sudden steps or frozen values. The reference unit remained on mains power, while the portable units ran on their internal batteries. The slowest sensor reached 90% of the reference change in 74 seconds; the fastest required 41 seconds. These response times were acceptable for the planned room-to-room survey, which focused on ventilation trends rather than second-by-second exposure. The calibration record was printed and attached to the equipment case. Students were instructed to repeat a short co-location check before every major field session and to note any unusual delay, battery warning or blocked vent. The procedure did not turn inexpensive monitors into laboratory instruments, but it established a traceable baseline for the course. More importantly, it gave students a clear example of why measurement quality depends on preparation, environmental control and careful documentation, not only on the number shown on a display
